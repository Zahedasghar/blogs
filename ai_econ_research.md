## Introduction
Artificial Intelligence (AI) is no longer peripheral to economics—it is reshaping labor markets, governance, and firm dynamics at their core. For developing countries, the speed of adoption poses both promise and peril. AI’s diffusion may amplify productivity, efficiency, and decision quality; yet, without inclusive design, it can exacerbate unemployment, inequality, and accountability deficits. This paper proposes an analytical framework and three key research questions focused on understanding how AI transforms economies like Pakistan’s.

---

## 1. AI and the Job Market: Augmentation or Displacement?

**Research Question:** Does generative and agentic AI complement or substitute human labor in services-heavy and informal economies?

**Key Hypotheses:**
- In the short run, AI acts as a *complement*, enhancing productivity and task accuracy for knowledge and digital workers.
- Over time, it becomes a *substitute* in routine cognitive jobs—BPOs, accounting, and content moderation—causing labor polarization.
- Gains accrue unequally to urban, educated workers; women benefit from flexible work only where connectivity and care burdens are addressed.

**Empirical Approach:**
- Combine household survey data (HIES, LFS) with digital labor metrics (Upwork, Fiverr) to construct an **AI-exposure index** by occupation.
- Identify causal impacts via *difference-in-differences* on firms adopting AI tools or *instrumental variables* based on task-level automation potential.

**Policy Implications:**
Governments must pivot training and digital skilling toward *AI-complementary* skills—data reasoning, ethics, communication, and adaptive learning—rather than generic coding.

---

## 2. AI in Policymaking: Efficiency vs. Accountability

**Research Question:** When does AI improve policy delivery without undermining accountability or equity?

**Key Hypotheses:**
- *Traditional AI* improves stable, rule-based systems (eligibility checks, targeting), while *agentic AI* excels in adaptive governance (disaster relief, fraud detection).
- Without transparency and grievance mechanisms, algorithmic efficiency risks eroding public trust.

**Proposed Framework:**
- Develop a **Policy Effectiveness and Fairness Index (PEFI)**: measuring accuracy, inclusion/exclusion errors, response time, appeals, and transparency logs.
- Use natural experiments from staggered AI adoption across ministries or RCTs comparing manual vs. AI-based targeting.

**Policy Implications:**
Institutional design must embed *human-in-the-loop* oversight, auditable decision logs, and citizen feedback to preserve democratic accountability.

---

## 3. AI, Firm Dynamics, and Market Concentration

**Research Question:** Does AI adoption promote SME productivity and formalization, or does it reinforce concentration among large firms?

**Key Hypotheses:**
- AI-driven tools lower costs and expand exports for SMEs using marketing and analytics automation.
- Data and compute barriers may centralize gains among big firms and global platforms.

**Empirical Strategy:**
- Examine firm-level TFP (Total Factor Productivity) and survival across firms receiving AI training vouchers or e-invoice mandates.
- Estimate heterogeneous treatment effects by size, sector, and digital maturity.

**Policy Implications:**
Developing economies need *open AI ecosystems*—accessible APIs, compute credits, and regulatory sandboxes—to democratize productivity gains.

---

## Cross-Cutting Disruptions
- **Task Unbundling:** Routine work shifts to models; humans focus on judgment and interaction.
- **Institutional Plumbing:** Governance becomes co-produced by humans and algorithms.
- **Data Inequality:** Access to compute and training data defines the next divide.
- **Skill Policy:** AI literacy must evolve into *AI adaptability*—critical thinking in dynamic, model-rich environments.

---

## Conclusion
AI is redefining economics as both subject and method—shifting how we measure productivity, equity, and governance itself. For developing countries, the challenge is to harness AI for inclusive growth, while avoiding the trap of algorithmic inequality. The research questions proposed here form a starting point for rigorous, policy-relevant inquiry into how automation, data, and agency interact to reshape the foundations of development economics.
